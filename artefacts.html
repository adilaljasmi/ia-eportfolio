Unit 1: Introduction to Agent-Based Computing

Collaborative Discussion 1 · Initial post

The unit looked at the trends in computing behind the rise of agents and at what makes a program autonomous. In my initial post I argued that agents emerged from five long-term trends in computing, namely ubiquity, interconnection, intelligence, delegation and human-orientation (Wooldridge, 2009). I also argued that agent-oriented decomposition suits complex, distributed problems (Jennings, 2000). I set out four organisational benefits (robustness and scalability, flexibility, decision support and efficiency) and ended with a caution: interactions between autonomous agents can produce unpredictable outcomes, so agents should be used where problems are genuinely distributed and dynamic.

Initial post (full screenshot)

Reflection (Rolfe et al., 2001)

What? I wrote my first post on why agent-based systems emerged and what they offer organisations, building it mainly on foundational sources.

So what? The benefits I listed came from the literature and were stated in general terms. Only the final paragraph asked when they might not hold. Looking back, the post described agents well but did not yet test the claims against real cases.

Now what? I want my later posts to pair each claimed benefit with evidence of where it fails. This became the main thread of the discussion in Units 2 and 3.

Unit 2: Introducing First Order Logic

Collaborative Discussion 1 · Peer responses

The seminar introduced first-order logic, the notation behind agents' knowledge representation, which I later used directly when writing KIF content in Unit 6. In the discussion I replied to three peers, each time proposing preventive measures rather than only agreeing:

  • Resilience of decentralised control: an advisory agent with a global view, like the staff holons in the PROSA architecture (Van Brussel et al., 1998), that can warn of deadlock without becoming a central point of failure. I also suggested transfer-of-control strategies with deadlines for when no human is available to approve (Scerri, Pynadath & Tambe, 2002).
  • Emergent failure: using the 2010 Flash Crash (Kirilenko et al., 2017), I argued that decentralisation swaps one failure mode for another. I proposed system-level circuit breakers, model checking of BDI programs before deployment (Bordini et al., 2006), and trust and reputation models for open systems (Ramchurn, Huynh & Jennings, 2004).
  • Accountability: using Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024), where the airline was held liable for its chatbot's advice, I argued for proportionate rather than limited oversight, with least-privilege tool access (OWASP Foundation, 2024).

Reflection (Rolfe et al., 2001)

What? I replied to three peers and grounded each reply in a concrete incident or architecture.

So what? Answering other people's arguments pushed me from describing benefits to designing safeguards. Each reply ended with a measure that could actually be built: a monitor, a verification step or a permission limit. The Air Canada case was especially useful, because it showed that autonomy can be delegated but accountability cannot.

Unit 3: Agent Architectures

Collaborative Discussion 1 · Summary post

The unit compared symbolic, reactive and belief-desire-intention (BDI) architectures. In my summary post I brought the discussion together. Peers had added the main agent models, namely reactive architectures that reject symbolic world models (Brooks, 1991), BDI agents (Rao & Georgeff, 1995) and hybrids, so the right choice depends on the environment. My main revision was that the benefits of agents are conditional, and the summary listed the safeguards our exchanges had identified. I concluded that autonomy is a design parameter rather than a default.

Summary post

Reflection (Rolfe et al., 2001)

What? I summarised a discussion that had changed my original position.

So what? Comparing my initial post with the summary shows the change: I started by listing benefits and finished by stating the conditions under which they hold. Reading peers' choices of agent model also made clear that architecture selection is a trade-off between responsiveness and deliberation, not a matter of picking the newest approach.

Now what? When I design an agent, I will justify the architecture against the environment it must work in. Our team did this in Unit 6, choosing a hybrid design with one deliberative planner and reactive specialists.

Unit 4: Hybrid Agent Architectures

Wiki contribution · “Layered Architecture”

I wrote the glossary entry on layered architectures, the most common way to build a hybrid agent. It contrasts horizontal layering (every layer sees input and proposes actions, as in TouringMachines (Ferguson, 1992), and needs a mediator to resolve conflicts) with vertical layering (control passes through the layers, as in InteRRaP (Müller, 1996), so there are fewer interactions but less fault tolerance). It also mentions the three-layer (3T) architecture used in robotics (Gat, 1998), and notes that layered designs can be hard to verify because behaviour emerges from interactions between layers.

Wiki entry: Layered Architecture

Reflection (Rolfe et al., 2001)

What? I produced a concise, referenced wiki entry explaining how hybrid agents are organised into layers.

So what? Writing for a shared glossary forced me to explain the trade-offs precisely. Horizontal layering risks a mediator bottleneck, while vertical layering fails if any one layer fails. The same tension appeared in our team design: the shared blackboard decouples the agents but concentrates risk in one component.

Now what? In future designs I will name the single point of failure explicitly and state how it is mitigated. Our design proposal did this for the blackboard, using atomic SQLite transactions and a bounded step budget.

Unit 5: Agent Communication

Collaborative Discussion 2 · Initial post

The unit introduced speech act theory, ontologies and agent communication languages (ACLs). The discussion asked how ACLs such as KQML compare with method invocation in languages like Java or Python. In my initial post I explained that KQML separates what a message is meant to do (its performative, such as ask-one or tell) from what it is about. Performatives come from speech act theory, which treats utterances as actions (Searle, 1969), and KQML organises messages into content, message and communication layers (Finin et al., 1994).

  • Advantages: interoperability between heterogeneous agents; autonomy, because a performative is a request that the receiver may refuse, unlike a method call (Wooldridge, 2009); and loose coupling through facilitators.
  • Disadvantages: ambiguous semantics in the original specification (Labrou, Finin & Peng, 1999); mental-state semantics that an observer cannot verify (Wooldridge, 2000), for which Singh (1998) proposed social commitments instead; and the overhead of parsing and shared ontologies.
  • Today: the debate has returned with LLM agents. Anthropic's Model Context Protocol standardises tool access, which resembles method invocation, while Google's Agent2Agent protocol gives tasks a lifecycle in which the remote agent can fail or ask for more input, echoing KQML (Anthropic, 2024; Google, 2025).

I concluded that ACLs are worth their overhead only where autonomy and heterogeneity are genuine requirements.

Initial post (full screenshot)

Reflection (Rolfe et al., 2001)

What? I compared ACLs with method invocation and linked the comparison to current LLM agent protocols.

So what? The clearest way to frame the difference was to ask who decides. With method invocation the caller decides, while with an ACL the receiver decides whether to act. Finding the same design question in protocols released in 2024 and 2025 showed me that agent communication theory is not only historical.

Now what? When I design interfaces between agents, I will first decide whether the receiver is genuinely independent. If it is, I will use structured messages with explicit refusal and failure replies rather than plain function calls.

Unit 6: Working Together

Collaborative Discussion 2 · Peer responses · e-Portfolio activity: creating agent dialogues (KQML and KIF) · team design proposal

The seminar put agent communication languages into practice by developing simple dialogues with the common KQML performatives, and compared the strengths and weaknesses of different approaches to agent communication.

Discussion 2: peer responses

I replied to three peers, each time proposing measures against the weaknesses they had raised:

  • Misinterpretation in a blackboard design: use KQML's own parameters (:language, :ontology, :reply-with and :in-reply-to) so that every claim can be traced (Finin et al., 1994). Make the shared vocabulary an explicit ontology with validation (Gruber, 1993), constrain conversations with interaction protocols (FIPA, 2002b), and add a control component that decides which agent acts next (Nii, 1986).
  • Incompatible KQML dialects: a single standard with formal semantics (FIPA, 2002a), observable social-commitment semantics (Singh, 1998) and conformance testing. I also noted that remote method calls can fail partially, which narrows the gap between method invocation and ACLs (Waldo et al., 1994).
  • LLM replies breaking a JSON schema (18 of 56 first replies in the peer's system): constrained decoding (Willard & Louf, 2023), with the caution that strict formats can reduce reasoning quality (Tam et al., 2024); an explicit “failed” reply, like KQML's sorry and error; and semantic checks, because a valid format does not guarantee correct meaning.

Activity: creating agent dialogues in KQML and KIF

Task: write a KQML/KIF dialogue in which Alice, a procurement agent, asks Bob, a warehouse stock agent, about the available stock of 50-inch televisions and their number of HDMI slots.

I wrote an eight-message dialogue. Alice uses ask-all to find every 50-inch model and its stock, ask-if with a KIF quantifier to check that at least 100 units are available, and ask-one to retrieve the HDMI slots for each model. Bob answers each with tell, linked to the question by :in-reply-to. To check that the dialogue is well formed, I built a small Python simulation in which Bob answers from a KIF knowledge base (the stock figures are illustrative). I then ran eight experiments:

  • An unknown model returned sorry: Bob understood the question but had no answer.
  • A malformed message, a different ontology or a misspelt relation (hdmi-slots instead of hdmi-ports) returned error.
  • ask-one returned only one of the two 50-inch models, so a procurement agent using it would underestimate stock.
  • After a simulated shipment, the same ask-if changed from tell to untell.

Reflection (Rolfe et al., 2001)

What? I replied to three peers on the weaknesses of ACLs, then wrote and tested my own KQML/KIF dialogue.

So what? The discussion and the activity pointed to the same lesson: a shared protocol fixes the form of messages, not their meaning. In the discussion I recommended explicit ontologies and failure replies. In my simulation, a misspelt relation produced an error even though both agents used KQML and KIF (Finin et al., 1994). The JSON-schema case showed the same problem returning at the boundary with language models, which our ScholarPlan design handled by requiring schema-valid JSON between agents.

Now what? In team projects I will agree shared message schemas and vocabularies early and test them with edge cases, rather than assuming a common protocol is enough.

Unit 7: Natural Language Processing (NLP)

Collaborative Discussion 2 · Summary post

The lecturecast introduced natural language processing: why human language is hard for machines to understand, and the methods and technologies used to process it. My Discussion 2 summary drew the discussion together under two findings. First, the boundary between the two approaches is less sharp than I had suggested. Method calls across a network face partial failure and timeouts, much as ACL messages do (Waldo et al., 1994). One peer's team chose plain Python calls but still recreated an ACL at the language-model boundary, with a JSON schema acting as the content language, and A2A's task lifecycle follows the same logic (Google, 2025). Second, semantics remains the central weakness. I listed the measures we had identified: recording ontology and conversation links in every message (Finin et al., 1994), interaction protocols that restrict valid replies (FIPA, 2002b), constrained decoding with its trade-offs (Willard & Louf, 2023; Tam et al., 2024), and explicit failure messages with semantic checks (Singh, 1998).

Summary post

Reflection (Rolfe et al., 2001)

What? I summarised a discussion that reframed my original comparison.

So what? My initial post treated ACLs and method invocation as competing technologies. By the end I saw the choice as one of trust boundaries. Method invocation suits components under one design authority, while ACL-style structure earns its overhead where the receiver is independent and its replies cannot be trusted. Language models belong in the second group, even inside a single system.

Now what? My Unit 11 implementation already reflects this: every Planner decision in ScholarPlan must pass a JSON schema and then a semantic check before the system acts on it, and a deterministic fallback takes over if the model fails twice. In future systems I will treat model outputs as untrusted messages from the start.

Unit 8: Understanding Natural Language Processing (NLP)

Seminar: Word2Vec worked example · e-Portfolio activity: creating constituency-based parse trees

The seminar covered the theories behind NLP, with a worked Word2Vec example in Python and a formative activity on how parse tree structures are created.

Word2Vec worked example

I implemented Word2Vec's skip-gram with negative sampling myself in NumPy (Mikolov et al., 2013a; 2013b) and trained it on a small synthetic corpus about animals, fruit and vehicles. Without being told the topics, the model placed every topic word nearest to a word from the same topic (mean cosine similarity 0.996 within topics and 0.628 between them). Experiments showed that removing very frequent words gave the clearest separation (gap 0.369 to 0.606). An ambiguous word (jaguar, used as both an animal and a car) received a single vector that blended both senses. My first run diverged because of a NumPy view bug, which I found, fixed and documented.

Activity: constituency-based parse trees

Task: create constituency parse trees for The government raised interest rates, The internet gives everyone a voice and The man saw the dog with the telescope.

Rather than only drawing the trees, I wrote a small context-free grammar and a parser that returns every tree the grammar allows, and then plotted them. The first two sentences have one tree each: interest rates is a noun compound, and gives takes two objects. The third sentence has two trees. If with the telescope attaches to the verb phrase, the man uses the telescope; if it attaches to the noun phrase, the dog has it. In experiments, removing any one grammar rule left a sentence with no parse. Adding further prepositional phrases increased the number of trees from 1 to 429, exactly the Catalan numbers reported by Church and Patil (1982).

Reflection (Rolfe et al., 2001)

What? I built Word2Vec from scratch and produced the parse trees, verifying both in code.

So what? Both activities showed the same limit from two sides: the ambiguous sentence has two valid parse trees, and the ambiguous word gets one blended vector. Word2Vec measures similarity of usage, not meaning; barn ended up next to bowl simply because both follow “in the”. The third sentence showed that syntax alone cannot decide meaning. A language-processing agent needs context or statistics about which words usually go together to choose between parses (Hindle & Rooth, 1993). The growth experiment showed how quickly this becomes a practical problem.

Now what? When an agent reads natural language, I will not treat a single parse as the truth. I will design it to detect ambiguity and resolve it with context, or to ask for clarification.

Unit 9: Introduction to Adaptive Algorithms

Collaborative Discussion 3 · Initial post · Additional task: an ethical or safety issue in intelligent agents

The unit introduced artificial neural networks and deep learning, their strengths and weaknesses. Collaborative Discussion 3 asked whether generative deep learning technologies raise ethical issues. In my initial post I argued that they do, and that the scale and low cost of generation make old problems bigger. I set out four issues: bias and representational harm from web-scale training data (Bender et al., 2021); fabrication, known as hallucination (Ji et al., 2023), illustrated by Mata v. Avianca (2023), where lawyers were sanctioned for filing court citations invented by ChatGPT; consent and copyright (Samuelson, 2023); and environmental cost, where Strubell, Ganesh and McCallum (2019) estimated that training one large model with neural architecture search emitted roughly five times the lifetime emissions of an average car. I argued that responsibility is shared, because developers choose the data, safeguards and release strategy while users must verify outputs, and that transparency rules such as Article 50 of the EU AI Act (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2024) do not address bias, consent or energy use on their own, so practices such as datasheets for datasets are still needed (Gebru et al., 2021).

Discussion 3 initial post

Additional task: excessive agency – limiting what an agent is allowed to do

For this task I researched responsible deployment, specifically what OWASP calls excessive agency (LLM06 in its 2025 Top 10). It lets an LLM-based system take damaging actions when the model's output is unexpected, ambiguous or manipulated, for example by prompt injection. OWASP traces it to three causes: too much functionality, too many permissions or too much autonomy (OWASP Foundation, 2024).

Its example is an email assistant that only needs to summarise messages but can also send them, so a malicious email can make it forward sensitive data. The recommended controls are design choices, not better models: few, narrow tools, minimum permissions, human approval for high-impact actions, and authorisation enforced in downstream systems rather than by the LLM.

This matters because LLMs will sometimes be wrong, and the harm depends on what the agent is allowed to do, which the deployer decides. Deployers are also held responsible for what their AI says: in Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024) the tribunal held the airline liable for its chatbot's misleading advice, rejecting the argument that the chatbot was responsible for its own actions.

In ScholarPlan, my multi-agent research assistant, each agent is a role with explicit permissions, following the Gaia methodology (Wooldridge, Jennings & Kinny, 2000). The agents share a SQLite blackboard and never call each other. Each agent writes through a role-scoped view of the blackboard that raises a permission error for any write outside its role: if the Retriever tried to write a verdict, it would be refused. The Retriever and the Archivist use no LLM, and only the Archivist writes the output files (report, BibTeX file, index and trace). The three agents that use an LLM contain no file-writing code, so their output can only reach the blackboard tables their role may write to.

Some controls concern people rather than code. The research goal goes to a third-party provider, so the README asks users not to enter personal data. API keys stay out of version control, traces record only relative paths so they do not reveal my account name, and each report states that it is AI-generated and checked only against abstracts.

One limitation is that permissions only cover writing; the Planner still decides alone how to break down the goal. A next step is letting the user approve the plan before retrieval. If ScholarPlan ever gained tools with real side effects, such as emailing results, that approval would no longer be optional.

Has anyone added a human approval step to their agent, and how did you decide which actions needed one?

Reflection (Rolfe et al., 2001)

What? I argued that generative deep learning raises four ethical issues and that responsibility is shared, and for the additional task I researched excessive agency and mapped it onto my own system.

So what? The two pieces looked at risk from different sides. My Discussion 3 post focused on what models produce; the excessive-agency research made me look at what my own agents are allowed to do. Checking ScholarPlan honestly also showed gaps: permissions only cover writing, and the Planner decides alone how to split the goal.

Now what? I will review every agent I build against the OWASP controls before release, and add a user approval step before any action with real side effects. I also need to follow regulation as it changes: after posting, I found that the 2026 amendment to the EU AI Act gave generative systems already on the market until 2 December 2026 to meet the content-marking duty (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2026).

Unit 10: Deep Learning in Action

Collaborative Discussion 3 · Peer responses · e-Portfolio activity: Deep Learning in Action

The seminar looked at the future direction of these technologies, including their ethical, social and economic issues.

Discussion 3: peer responses

I replied to three peers, each time proposing safeguards that could actually be built:

  • Human approval that is too easy to give: a peer had approved a paper with no DOI at their own checkpoint. I described this as automation bias (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010), noted that the EU AI Act requires high-risk systems to be designed so that their human overseers can stay aware of it, an obligation that applies from 2 December 2027 for most high-risk uses after the 2026 amendment (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2024; 2026), and proposed cognitive forcing functions (Buçinca, Malaya & Gajos, 2021), resolving identifiers through doi.org, alerts when an unusually large share of records is excluded, and the ability to contest single decisions (Alfrink et al., 2023).
  • From principles to practice: principles alone do not prevent harm (Mittelstadt, 2019). I suggested published bias audits, since text-to-image models amplified stereotypes even when asked for counter-stereotypes (Bianchi et al., 2023), reported in model cards (Mitchell et al., 2019), and consent checks and marking for voice cloning, after the FCC confirmed in 2024 that AI-generated voices count as ‘artificial’ under US robocall law, so such calls need prior consent (Federal Communications Commission, 2024).
  • A critic built on a language model: LLMs struggle to correct their own reasoning (Huang et al., 2024) and tend to favour their own generations (Panickssery, Bowman & Feng, 2024), and retrieval-based legal tools still hallucinated in 17% to 33% of queries (Magesh et al., 2025). I proposed external rule-based checks, claim-level verification and a named reviewer for each output.

Activity: Deep Learning in Action – AlphaFold

Overview

I chose AlphaFold, a deep learning system from Google DeepMind that predicts a protein's 3D structure from its amino acid sequence. Knowing a protein's structure helps explain how it works, but solving one experimentally can take months to years, and only around 100,000 unique structures had been determined against billions of known sequences (Jumper et al., 2021).

In the CASP14 assessment AlphaFold's median backbone error was 0.96 Å, against 2.8 Å for the next best method (Jumper et al., 2021). Its predictions are free in the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database, built with EMBL-EBI, which grew from over 360,000 structures to over 214 million by 2024 (Varadi et al., 2022; Varadi et al., 2024). In 2024 Demis Hassabis and John Jumper shared half of the Nobel Prize in Chemistry ‘for protein structure prediction’ (The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, 2024).

How it works

AlphaFold 2's input is the sequence plus a multiple sequence alignment (MSA) of related sequences (Jumper et al., 2021). Its core is a stack of repeated Evoformer blocks, which use attention and other layers to pass information between the MSA and a map of residue pairs, so the model can reason about evolutionary and spatial relationships together. A structure module then builds 3D coordinates by giving each residue a rotation and translation, and the whole network is applied repeatedly (‘recycling’) to refine the prediction.

Each prediction includes a per-residue confidence score, pLDDT (0 to 100). Above 90 is generally high accuracy, while below 50 often indicates a disordered region (Varadi et al., 2024).

Impacts

On the positive side, AlphaFold 2 has been used by more than two million people in 190 countries, including work on antibiotic resistance and enzymes that break down plastic (The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, 2024).

The main risk I see is over-trust. Terwilliger et al. (2024) found that even very high-confidence predictions sometimes differed from experimental maps, and they recommend treating predictions as hypotheses to be checked experimentally. They also note that only 36% of residues in the human proteome are predicted with very high confidence, although many low-confidence regions are likely to be disordered.

Control is another issue. AlphaFold 3 was published without its code, and DeepMind had partnered with Alphabet's drug-development company Isomorphic Labs (Nature, 2024). The inference code followed in November 2024 and is now open source (Google DeepMind, 2026), but the downloadable weights are still limited to non-commercial organisations (Google DeepMind, 2024). AI drug design also carries a dual-use risk: a drug-discovery model (not AlphaFold) retargeted towards toxicity generated about 40,000 candidate molecules, including the nerve agent VX, in under six hours (Urbina et al., 2022).

Do you think restricting the AlphaFold 3 weights is a sensible safeguard against misuse, or does it mostly protect commercial interests?

Reflection (Rolfe et al., 2001)

What? I replied to three peers with concrete safeguards and wrote my Deep Learning in Action post on AlphaFold.

So what? The peer responses kept returning to one idea: a safeguard is only as good as the person or model operating it. Researching AlphaFold showed the same pattern outside language models, because even an accurate system needs its confidence scores read critically. It was also a useful contrast with Discussion 3: AlphaFold's main social risks come from over-trust and from who controls access, rather than from fabricated content.

Now what? When I use or build predictive systems, I will show confidence measures to users and treat outputs as hypotheses until they are checked.

Unit 11: Intelligent Agents in Action

Collaborative Discussion 3 · Summary post · individual project (ScholarPlan)

The unit looked at agents in practice, in smart manufacturing and in agent-based modelling for finance. The individual implementation of ScholarPlan was due at the end of this unit. In my Discussion 3 summary I said the discussion had changed my answer. My initial post had assumed that human review was a reliable safeguard, but peers showed how fragile it is: automation bias leads people to accept automated outputs and miss errors (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010), a model-based critic may share the blind spots of the model it checks (Huang et al., 2024), and principles do not prevent harm without concrete mechanisms (Mittelstadt, 2019). I listed the measures we had identified (cognitive forcing functions, external rule-based checks, the ability to contest individual decisions and published bias audits) and concluded that the question is not only who is responsible, but whether the people made responsible can genuinely exercise that responsibility.

Discussion 3 summary post

Reflection (Rolfe et al., 2001)

What? I summarised Discussion 3 after building my individual ScholarPlan implementation.

So what? The discussion moved my answer from ‘who is responsible’ to ‘can the responsible person actually catch errors’. My own system showed why: ScholarPlan's Critic already had a rule-based pre-check, but it only confirmed that cited claim IDs were verified, so in its first live run an uncited bridging sentence in the synthesis was accepted (README, section 11). Unit 12: The Future of Intelligent Agents

Seminar · the future of the technology from Unit 10

The final seminar asked us to discuss where the technology we wrote about in Unit 10 is heading. These were my notes.

The future of AlphaFold

AlphaFold, which I wrote about in Unit 10, is moving from single proteins towards whole molecular systems. AlphaFold 3 predicts complexes containing proteins, nucleic acids, small molecules, ions and modified residues, and is more accurate on protein–ligand interactions than specialised docking tools (Abramson et al., 2024). Because many medicines are small molecules that bind to proteins, this is the step that matters most for drug discovery.

The open problems point to the next research directions. AlphaFold 3 predicts static structures, not how molecules move in solution, and running it with different random seeds does not approximate that movement; it can also produce ‘hallucinations’ in disordered regions (Abramson et al., 2024). Its authors expect further gains as experimental methods such as cryo-electron microscopy supply new training data, so experiments and prediction are likely to keep improving each other rather than one replacing the other.

The social question changes as prediction is built into automated design pipelines. Someone still has to decide when a prediction is good enough to act on, and experimental checking (Terwilliger et al., 2024) then becomes both a bottleneck and a safeguard.

Discussion questions: (1) Will predicting molecular motion need a different kind of model, or mainly more experimental data? (2) In an automated drug-design pipeline, who should sign off that a prediction is reliable enough to act on?

Reflection (Rolfe et al., 2001)

What? I prepared notes on where AlphaFold is heading for the final seminar.

So what? Looking forward made me weigh capability against verification: the technology is extending to whole molecular systems, but its limits on motion and disorder mean experiments remain essential.

Now what? When I build agents that act on model predictions, I will decide in advance who checks a prediction, and how, before anything is done with it.
